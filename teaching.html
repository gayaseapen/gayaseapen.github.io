Teaching Philosophy

My teaching consists of pedagogical experiments across digital technology studies, sound and visual storytelling, and critical making. Across these areas, I encourage students to grapple with the fundamentals of digital technologies and media. This has involved tinkering with code as well as components that constitute digital devices and infrastructures, such as circuits and sensors. In light of a renewed emphasis on computational systems and platforms, following the popularization of generative AI tools in everyday life, I orient students to emerging technologies through a combination of pedagogical methods, including critical making and creative production.
In my courses, I attempt to scaffold the technical through distinct forms of storytelling, leading to production-intensive classroom environments. A large part of this process involves developing familiarity with cameras, sound recorders, editing software, code-writing and editing tools, and fabrication studios. However, the more crucial learning involves reflecting on the choice of stories to be narrated; the role of media technologies in shaping those narratives; and the inescapable role of platforms in shaping distinct forms of expression. To develop these competencies, I draw from scholarship and pedagogical practices from a range of intersecting fields, including digital humanities, media production, digital media studies, media anthropology, and critical code and software studies, among others.
Instead of feeling the burden of having to create a perfect project or product, I encourage students toward practice-oriented research that involves iterative feedback, collaborative building, and personal mentoring. In what follows, I showcase a range of student-produced projects completed in my courses that apply these teaching methods. Several of these works have been presented in showcases within the university and beyond. Graduates who have developed these skills and competencies now work in a range of industries, including sound and music production, web development, nonprofits, and social media management.

Critical AI-led Storytelling

I approach AI–including machine learning, training and recommender algorithms, and LLMs–by embracing the interactive space between humans and machine-based code. In my web-based storytelling courses, we work across HTML, CSS, and JavaScript, recognizing the need for consistent data structures, linking, and prompt engineering. Alongside this, we also work on solutions for circumventing access concerns around hosting, paywalls, and tokens using self-hosted projects and free and open-source tools.

Despite the promises of vibe coding and AI-generated content, I see technical training as insufficient for working with digital creation tools. For instance, there is an increasing proliferation of online libraries and tools (UI/UX libraries) that are used in AI-assisted coding and promise to generate faster results. However, while teaching an introductory course on web-based storytelling, I have found that these tools often lead substantial debugging (correction and rewriting) time to fix inconsistencies. This becomes especially acute if students struggle with the primary code architecture. The same goes for third-party and pre-existing embed tools. I often have to remind students that they might not get the desired results during the first attempt using an AI tool, and that a back-and-forth search process mirrors the practice of using an encyclopaedia or a reference manual, with the added perks of speed and natural language processing (NLP).

Further, I approach AI using critical code and software studies approaches that include engaging with the tools that have brought about AI in the first place. During a module on the simple web-design tool, Markdown, students and I discussed the hierarchies, limitations, and role of the language in shaping platforms and internet cultures, including text mining for large language models (LLMs). Additionally, students also learned to use Markdown to create web pages and other digital outputs that work with text as a primary format.

In such sessions and others, demystifying “AI” and introducing its various facets involves a three-pronged approach for me. First, we engage with the “thought” around AI: decoding concepts of intelligence; the promises and mysteries of artificial general intelligence (AGI); and the histories of computing and internet cultures that shape the hype around AI in our present moment. Second, we delve into the “practice” of using AI tools: engaging with the tools for creating, editing, and generating. For instance, in trying out image generation platforms like Adobe Firefly or AI-based sound libraries, we reflect on both the qualities and the limitations of AI-generated content. Finally, we reckon with the implications of the use of generative AI, including the ability to speed up and scale processes across time and space. These attempts have led to productive discussions about the spread of misinformation, the proliferation of hate and polarized speech on digital platforms, and the copious gathering and processing of personal data across domains and industries.

Multimedia Production

In a visually saturated world, my courses aim to begin the process of making sense of the content that surrounds us while also learning to tell important and timely stories. Having taught courses across different levels that involve developing production competencies–from introductory to advanced–I approach sound and visual media through smaller constituent units. Crafting feature-length projects, then, involves first grasping the process of recording and editing of smaller products. For instance, in my course on Sonic Storytelling (Fall 2024 and 2025), we worked on modules on “writing sound” and critical listening; recording sound using mobile recorders; and editing and producing through scripts and software editors. Instead of learning just to record voice, students incorporated ambient and feature sounds they recorded to narrate rich and gripping stories. Alongside this work, we also engaged with critical thought and writing about what constitutes “sound” and “noise” in distinct cultural environments. We explored how sound and knowledge are intertwined by viewing and reading Steven Feld’s work on the sounds of rainforests and the Kaluli people in Papua New Guinea. In the same course, we also attempted to understand how sounds have been codified and turned into databases and tools of machine listening by auditing databases and perusing artistic projects by collectives like machinelistening.exposed.




Old Media in New Worlds

"Old Media in New Worlds" is a podcast-series produced by students in DCD 303: Sonic Storytelling (Fall 2025) as a limited series that creatively juxtaposes the culture of old and new media forms using methods of sonic storytelling. Across distinct episodes of approximately 8-10 minutes, students observe how older media practices—ranging from CDs, photo albums, and arcade games—have either significantly shaped popular forms (like social media or online gaming) in the present, or have made a comeback as nostalgic or hobby-based practices. Inclusive of personal stories, research sources, and expert commentary, these episodes stand out for their unique use of sounds to embellish oral/aural modes of storytelling and expression, fit for the digital era.



While working with visual storytelling forms and production techniques, I attempt to combine learning from distinct visual epistemologies and multisensory formats. The camera, editing lab, and fabrication studios, then, complement the process of developing awareness of global circulation and production cultures; urgent social and civic issues in local communities; and interpersonal relations within local and regional communities.

Carnival, Cricket and Cypto: Media Cultures in Trinidad and Tobago (Summer 2026)

During a study-abroad program I co-lead with Prof. Jesse Willis (Musician) in Trinidad and Tobago, students and I had a chance to meet regional filmmakers, discuss digital infrastructures in the Carribean with journalist Mark Lindersay, and explore the history and legacy of cricket in defining the sport media infrastructure and cultures in the island country. Engaging with the work of historian and writer CLR James (Beyond the Boundary), we spoke to Prof. Douglas-Wade Brunton and discussed his work on Media, Cricket, and the West Indian identity. This unique experience was informative in expanding the contours of learning from beyond the American perspetive and account for the shaping of sports, media, and identity in a globalized world. We also attended steel-pan workshops with local panyards, led by the steel-pan musician Amrit Samroo. We also attended workshops on Tasa, an Indo-Trinidadian percussion form that has been shaped by the culture of creolization in Trinidad and Tobago. Listening and enjoying Chutney and Soca music was as close as it came to bringing my work on media cultures in India to my students from North America.

Counter Creating and Fabrication

Accounting for a diversity of learners and modes of learning, my classrooms offer a space for embodied learning instead of privileging mental cognition. Empowered by the work carried out in feminist design and code studies, I extend a practice of counter-creating that moves beyond the dominant modes of production, skilling, and learning. For instance, in my course on Social Media (Spring 2026), students worked on a digital garden project that emphasized curation and personal memory work. This involved experimenting with a range of tools for self-expression and documentation that operated outside the logics of platforms designed for algorithmic curation.


Photos from different making and fabrication sessions across courses. Photo Credit: Nathaniel Lapila, Karin Barrett, and Samaya Rochodi.


Several of my courses have involved working with circuit kits, sensors, and physical computing, especially in collaboration with the makerspace for technical support. In one of my courses introducing digital culture and design, we worked on a personal crafts and circuits module alongside reading excerpts from Jennifer Gabry’s How to do Things with Sensors. In other courses, making projects have led to an extension of the possibilities of digital storytelling through 3D printing, laser cutting, and embroidery, thereby repurposing spaces meant for prototyping and STEM innovation. In one such course, Technology and Humanity, we worked on repair as a learning praxis, developing competencies to carry out small repair projects. Students worked on projects ranging from 3-D printing TV remote covers to embroidering patches on thrifted clothing items. Here, we approached repair as a critical framework for studying emerging technologies. We looked at projects like Geographies of Digital Waste and read Jane Hutton’s work on reciprocal landscapes, as well as multisensory ethnographies like Smellscaping Guiyu. We also moved beyond “making” as an extension of a thought exercise; instead, we engaged with the work of repair communities and international “right to repair” movements and built awareness of the afterlives of trash, particularly tech trash, and its implications for the landscapes, bodies, and communities that find themselves on the receiving end of technology design.
